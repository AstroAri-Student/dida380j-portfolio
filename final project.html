Campus News

Birds are dying en masse on campus. Here's what we learned.

New data shows the worst offenders on campus.

By ARI WHITE - December 10, 2025

Disclaimer: This article contains images of dead birds in various states of decay, which some readers may find graphic or disturbing.

Thunk, goes your window, late at night. Thunk, thunk. Thunk. In dorms, the Library Tower, the Academic Buildings, all across campus, every day has the same persistent refrain. Thunk. Thunk. Thunk.

This is the sound of birds dying.

Window strikes are caused by glass reflecting their surroundings sharply enough that birds confuse that reflection for open air. Flying at high speeds, mistaking that reflection for a flyway, birds impact those windows fast enough to sometimes leave the impressions of their wings on the glass. Whether or not the pattern of their wings is left behind, they always have that same sound – thunk. The empty, hollow ring of collision, and a small body left broken on the earth. These incidents are incredibly harmful to bird populations, with an estimated annual death rate of up to one billion birds. Most occur during the two annual major migrations, one in fall, the other in spring, when huge swaths of the continent are covered by birds traveling between breeding grounds or to their seasonal haunts. This also means that many of the casualties are young birds, less than a year old, who are experiencing their first migration and have not yet learned to avoid windows.

Unfortunately, these impacts are typically not survivable. Songbirds fly at about 30 mph, or 13.5 m/s, during migration, which is significantly faster than their regular, day-to-day flight speed. While most deaths are not immediate, these impacts cause serious blunt force trauma and concussions in birds, which means their bodies may be found very far from the window that caused their death. New research has shown that 60% of window strikes result in death in the best-case scenario, when the bird is found immediately and taken to treatment. This percentage is likely much higher for birds who are not treated. Which means that, while we may find approximately one billion little bird bodies beneath our windows, the death toll is even higher than we thought.

That means nights like this one in Chicago are even worse than the heaps of bodies they leave behind would imply. That night, 964 dead birds were found along the edges of McCormick Place Lakeside Center; only 80 stunned living birds were found. This means that, potentially, a large, unknown number of birds who impacted the building flew away, only to succumb to their injuries elsewhere.

But while Chicago is a hotspot for migration deaths, it is far from the only offender. Binghamton University has its own death toll.

Through the combined efforts of the university’s Environmental Studies Department and the Binghamton Audubon Society, we have a new glimpse into the impact of the university’s building design on bird deaths in the area. Despite our reputation and accolades for sustainability, Binghamton University is a serious offender in the bird world, the center of numerous mass bird casualties. The dataset put together by the Environmental Studies Department and the Audubon Society contains 82 confirmed fatalities over the course of the past ten years, when the department began collecting the data. Like in many other places, the vast majority of strikes were recorded in autumn and spring, during the major migration events. Part of this is certainly sample bias; there are more people on campus during the spring and fall semesters, which means there are more potential witnesses able to report a strike than in other seasons. However, given that there are two incidents of an entire flock of cedar waxwings, totaling 18 birds, being taken out at once in the autumn, it’s safe to assume that the seasonal migration is as much a factor in the data as the number of witnesses.

The data also shows that the majority of birds are indeed migrationary songbirds, particularly warblers, sparrows, and thrushes. Warbler migrations are typically a source of immense joy for passionate birders, to the point that the long hours spent observing them has led to a condition commonly called warbler neck, due to the posture birders take to watch high-perching birds flitting between branches. Colorful, cheerful, and hard to identify, warblers are the bird watcher’s seasonal delight, and they are also a major window strike casualty.

Some buildings on campus are deadlier than others. According to the dataset, the worst offenders are Academic A, Science 3, and the Science Library (attached to Science 2) – buildings that are all panelled in glass. In these locations, more than half of all bird fatalities were found.

It should be noted that the only fatalities associated with Academic A are the cedar waxwing flock strikes. This may come as a surprise, given that the building is nothing but glass panels. This is because Academic A is such a dangerous building to birds that, according to Dr. Clarke, one of the leads in the bird strike project, Facilities Management prioritizes checking for and cleaning bird corpses around it, such that most students never see its outsized effect. The many workers and groundskeepers of the university are so aware of how deadly its buildings are, they have protocols in place to shield the students’ eyes from the brutality. The Binghamton Audubon Society is currently reaching out to Facilities Management in order to improve the window strike dataset with their findings, as previously the data was collected through student reports and a few dedicated graduate students.

These deaths are not caused by unknown effects. They are a result of the way the campus’s buildings are designed. The huge glass panels, often placed right beside trees or greenery, are the direct cause of these window strikes. Additionally, many of these buildings keep their lights on late into the night, which severely disrupt migrating birds who are drawn toward light sources. Warblers seeking shelter and safety, or seeing the reflections of trees or bushes where they can rest, instead meet cold, hard glass that shatters their skulls and leaves them dead or dying. None of this is necessary. All of it is preventable, if only we care enough to do something about it.

So, how can the university become a bit less deadly for birds? How can we silence the nightly thunk, thunk, thunk of birds dying against our windows? Must we replace all our windows with brickwork, shut off our lights, and live in darkness, cut off from the outside? Should we isolate ourselves away from the natural world that we so desperately love? Is the only solution to the human causes of bird deaths the removal of humans?

Obviously not. We students of Binghamton University love the natural world because we are part of it, and we will not solve its problems by taking ourselves out of that world. We have large windows because we love seeing outside, in part because we love seeing birds fly by. We have swathes of trees and greenery because we need it as much as the birds do. And we keep our lights on late in the night not just because we spend our late hours studying, but because we migrate toward light sources, too. We can keep all of these things and still make our campus safer for the creatures we love to watch.

And, fortunately, many of these solutions are cheap and easy. The Cornell Lab of Ornithology, developers of the birding apps Merlin Bird ID and EBird and fan-favorites of bird watchers worldwide, have a handy list of methods to bird-proof windows. Some of the simplest solutions include hanging ropes or paracord, spaced approximately 4 inches apart, outside of problem windows, or applying dots or decals spaced 2 inches apart to the glass. In fact, in the recent renovations being done across campus, some parts of the Science Buildings have already had their windows replaced with pre-decaled glass. The more buildings implement these simple measures, the more birds will be saved. Additionally, campus buildings need merely shut off their lights or shutter their windows at night to prevent nighttime strikes. Not only does this help save birds, but it also helps to save energy costs – running the lights in an empty building isn’t free, nor is it an efficient use of our tuition money.

And there's no reason not to have a little fun with it. Simple, evenly-spaced dots work well enough. But the campus community is talented, creative, artistic. With a few glass-safe markers and a couple of friends, we can transform our windows into works of art that make our campus more cheerful and more safe for wildlife. A campus-wide initiative to decorate our buildings with window murals could transform the university from a graveyard for birds into a bird-safe masterpiece.

With so little to lose, and so many saved bird lives to gain, why shouldn't we flock together and make our campus a bit bird-safer?
